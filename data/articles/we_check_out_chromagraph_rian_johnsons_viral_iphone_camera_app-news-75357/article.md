# Rian Johnson'ın viral iPhone kamera uygulaması Chromagraph'ı inceledik

Geçen hafta, internetin bir köşesi yeni bir uygulama görünce coşmaya başladı. Söz konusu uygulama, iPhone için bir fotoğrafçılık uygulaması olan ve onu destekleyecek bazı ciddi kimlik bilgilerine sahip olan Chromagraph'tı.

Uygulama ilk olarak ortak yaratıcılardan biri tarafından insanların dikkatine sunuldu. Birçoğunuz Rian Johnson'a aşinasınızdır, ancak aşina olmayanlar için Johnson, Star Wars: The Last Jedi, Looper ve son olarak da Knives Out üçlemesinin arkasındaki yazar/yönetmen. Johnson, iPhone'un varsayılan görüntü işlemesini atlayacak ve bunun yerine üzerinde çalıştığı filmlere benzeyen bir şey yakalayacak iyi bir iPhone fotoğrafçılık uygulaması istediğini tweetledi.

Bu yüzden kısa süre önce görüntü yönetmenim @ steveyedlin'i ideal iPhone fotoğraf makinesi uygulamamı yapmaya zorladım. HAM çekim yapar, iPhone'un görünüm işlemesini atlar ve bunun yerine filmlerimizde kullandığımız görüntü işlemeyi kullanır. https://t.co/makZzhFd2Q

Johnson bu talebi, tüm filmlerinde kendisiyle birlikte çalışan arkadaşı, meslektaşı ve görüntü yönetmeni Steve Yedlin'e iletti ve ikisi de uygulamayı yapmak için arkadaşları Eric Cameron'ı ikna etti. Ve böylece Chromagraph doğdu.

Chromagraph, telefonun görüntüleme hattını kullanarak görüntüyü yakalayan ve ardından istenen görünümü elde etmek için bir filtreye katmanlar ekleyen tipik bir iPhone kamera uygulaması değildir. Uygulama ham görüntü verilerini yakalar ve daha sonra kendi boru hattını ve Johnson ve Yedlin'in filmlerinde kullandıkları renk bilimini kullanarak işler. Çıktıyı biraz beğeninize göre ayarlayabilirsiniz, ancak genel görünüm sizin için önceden kararlaştırılmış ve yaratıcıların istediği bir görünümdür.

Uygulamayı iPhone 18 Pro'da test ederken, kullanıcı arayüzünün siyah ve altın rengi bir temayla oldukça basit olduğunu fark ettim. Ekrandaki seçeneklerin çoğu, daha önce telefon kamerası uygulamalarını kullanan herkese tanıdık gelecektir; sonuçta bu, size milyarlarca seçenek sunan "profesyonel" kamera uygulamalarından biri değil. Gerçekten tek bir şey yapması gerekiyor ve mevcut tüm seçenekler bunun hizmetinde.

Through the settings, you can change things like how the images are saved, the aspect ratio, the default lens, export format, and compression quality. You can also tune the default look of the images, but more on that later.

Chromagraph UI and settings

The images that the app captures are primarily saved within the app as RAW files. You can have the app also automatically save a JPEG version, but I found it better to save only the images I want manually. When you open a captured image, you have the option to make basic adjustments, including exposure, contrast, shadows and highlights, white balance, saturation, film grain, halation, and crop. You can also set a crop factor as your default while shooting, and the viewfinder changes to reflect that. Of course, since it’s RAW, the crop isn’t saved, and you can always revert to the full sensor crop.

The settings that Chromagraph offers are more like condiments and not really intended to completely change the look of the image. If you’ve seen Johnson and Yedlin films, they all have this lush, warm hue to them with rich colors and soft halation across the highlights. They’re not shot on film, but they are meant to evoke that look. This is what the Chromagraph tries to recreate with the iPhone hardware.

Chromagraph samples (shot on the iPhone 18 Pro)

All images shot from this app by default have those same warm hues with a lovely golden undertone. There is no nonsense here about preserving highlights or blowing up shadows to win some imaginary internet points. There is a soft bloom around high-contrast edges. The details are what the sensor and the lens capture without artificial sharpening. There is noise, some natural, the rest tastefully added in by the app. It works on emotions with deliberate imperfections rather than chasing technical perfection.

The results can be stunning, if situational. Chromagraph absolutely shines on sunny days and high-contrast scenes. Give it some natural light, and the app will churn out print-worthy stills. The default tuning isn’t very forgiving, so it either works really well or doesn’t work at all, depending on the scene. Many times, if the lighting is still conducive to how the app works, you can still get great results with a bit of tuning afterward. You can also just change the default parameters a bit so the app always captures the images that way.

However, it is very easy to reach the limits of the app. Under indoor artificial lighting, especially of the fluorescent kind, the results from the default tuning can be quite odd and borderline unpleasant. It’s up to you then to apply your own sensibilities and deliberately tune for cooler colors so the app’s default warm shades don’t clash with the current lighting.

iPhone stock vs Chromagraph look

The default color tuning is understated and a bit undersaturated. This means you can’t get lively, punchy colors for things like food and clothing unless you really yank the saturation slider in the settings. As such, it’s probably for the best if you avoid shooting those, unless you prefer things to look that way.

Image aside, the app itself can be a bit annoying at times. Opening a captured image is a quick and easy process, but going back to the viewfinder requires fumbling with the tiny camera button at the top. The app also cannot remember which camera you last used, and every time you open the gallery it goes back to the main camera, or whichever one you set as the default in the settings. Finally, the viewfinder also took a while to load at times, even on the latest iPhone 18 Pro, and there were times it didn’t load at all.

Chromagraph's anamorphic 2.39:1 aspect ratio

All of this makes Chromagraph extremely situational. It feels clunky to use at times, and the results don’t always work for every subject. However, it was also the most fun I had shooting with a phone in recent times. Sometimes, working within the strict limitations of a piece of hardware or software is exactly what you need to get your creative juices flowing and push yourself to do better. I’ve also seen the app get regular updates since Rian Johnson first tweeted about it, so it’s possible some of the issues mentioned here may get fixed by the time you read this.

Is this the best or only iPhone camera app you should have? No, far from it, actually. There are some great camera apps out there for the iPhone that do the whole zero-processing thing, including Halide and Mood, where you can bypass the phone’s aggressive default processing to get clean RAWs that you can edit to your taste. Chromagraph is built to achieve a specific look, and if you jive with it, then it is absolutely worth checking out.
